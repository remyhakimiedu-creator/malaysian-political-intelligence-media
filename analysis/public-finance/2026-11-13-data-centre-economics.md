# Data-Centre Boom — Public Finance & Economic Analysis

**As of:** 2026-11-13 | **Confidence:** High (BNM/MIDA figures); Low (fiscal cost — not public) | **Based on:** research/economy/2026-11-13-data-centre-boom.md

## Bottom line
[ANALYSIS] Data centres are now Malaysia's largest FDI magnet: 51% of net FDI inflows in 2024. Their direct GDP contribution is about 1%. Value retained is limited by heavy equipment imports, few operating jobs and future profit repatriation. Whether incentives are justified cannot be judged from public data, because the cost of the tax incentives has not been published.

## What it means [ANALYSIS]
- **Big capital, small payroll.** Data centres are mostly land, buildings, power systems and imported servers, so few people run them. High pay (BNM: RM3,000–30,000) does not offset the small numbers.
- **Leakage.** Imported equipment adds to capital imports rather than local output. Profits from foreign-owned facilities flow out through the primary income account (BNM). The net national gain is therefore smaller than the gross investment figures suggest.
- **Upside that is real but uncertain.** ICT services exports are rising. Local suppliers (racks, cooling) have started manufacturing here. Cheaper local cloud services may raise productivity for SMEs and government. These forward linkages are hard to measure, and BNM calls them the main source of gains.
- **Who pays for infrastructure.** The stated policy is "causer pays": separate tariffs, connected load charges, and investors funding transmission upgrades (Johari, Oct 2026). If it is enforced, TNB's grid capex for data centres should be recovered from them, not from households. That cannot yet be verified, because the tariff and cost-allocation details are not public.

## Trade-offs & distributional effects
| Group | Potential gain | Potential cost |
|---|---|---|
| Households | Indirect: jobs, digital services | Tariff and water risk if cost allocation fails; local disruption |
| SMEs / local suppliers | Construction, M&E, cooling, services contracts | Competition for power and land; supplier gap vs imports |
| Government | Investment headlines; some tax once incentive periods end | Revenue forgone; enforcement costs |
| TNB / utilities | Regulated returns on new capacity | Stranded assets if reservations go unused (partly hedged by take-or-pay) |
| Foreign operators | Location, incentives, cheaper power than Singapore | Rising tariffs, stricter rules, US compliance risk |

## Arguments on each side
- **"Incentives justified":** without them, capital would go to Thailand, Indonesia or Singapore. The FDI and spillovers would be lost. Incentives are now tied to efficiency (DESAC PUE/WUE).
- **"Incentives excessive":** the Treasury SG says "we perhaps overdid the red carpet". Location and demand already favoured Johor, so some incentives may have rewarded investment that would have come anyway (deadweight). [ANALYSIS: this cannot be tested without project-level data.]

## Uncertainties & data caveats
- No published tax expenditure figure for MD status or DESAC.
- The RM385.7 billion cumulative figure needs reconciling (calculation 4).
- Job numbers are projections, and actual employment is not reported.
